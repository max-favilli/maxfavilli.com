For most of my adult life a car meant a petrol car, and everything else was a variant. I have just switched to an electric one, and before writing anything about what that is like I wanted to know what everyone else is doing. So I went and got the registration figures — not a summary of them, the actual monthly releases, twenty-two of them, and pulled the numbers out by hand.

The story in them is not the one I expected, and it is not the one in most of the coverage.

## What actually happened

Every figure here is new car registrations — cars bought new in that month, not cars on the road. The distinction matters more than it sounds, and I will come back to it.

Battery-electric cars went from **14.4% of new EU registrations in October 2024 to 27.7% in August 2026**. Petrol went the other way, from 30.8% to 18.9%. Diesel continued its long retreat, 10.9% down to 6.4%. Taken across the whole of 2026 so far, petrol and battery-electric are now level — 21.7% each, a little over 1.6 million cars apiece.

That much is roughly what you would guess. The interesting part is the hybrids.

With a hybrid you get part of the electrification benefit without depending on chargers or worrying about range. You buy one instead of a BEV, and you tell yourself you will go electric next time. Through 2025 that was the obvious thing to do, and hybrids climbed steadily to more than a third of the market. In **February 2026 they peaked at 38.7%**.

They have fallen in every month since. By August they were at 33.2%, almost exactly where they had started twenty-two months earlier.

They are not the only ones falling, though, and this is where I had to correct myself. Between February and August hybrids gave up 5.5 points, petrol 4.2 and diesel 1.7. Battery-electric gained 9.4. So BEV is taking share from three directions at once, and hybrids are only one of them.

What the numbers show is not buyers switching from hybrid to electric. It is the hybrid losing its exemption — declining now alongside petrol and diesel, instead of holding as the durable middle option it was supposed to be.

## The road is not the showroom

Now the distinction I flagged at the start. Of all the cars actually on EU roads, battery-electric accounted for **2.3% in 2024** — the most recent year ACEA has published a fleet figure for. Not 27.7%. Two point three.

Both numbers are true and they are measuring different things. One is what people are buying this month; the other is what everyone is still driving. Cars stay on the road for well over a decade, so a quarter of new sales takes a very long time to become a quarter of the traffic.

## Two honest caveats

**The monthly line is noisy, and anyone can cherry-pick it.** December 2025 hit 22.6% as buyers pulled purchases forward; January 2026 fell straight back to 19.3% and February to 18.3%. If you want a headline saying electric sales are collapsing, February 2026 will give you one. If you want the opposite, August will. Neither month means anything on its own.

**There is a hole in July 2026.** ACEA takes a summer break and publishes no July release, so July's monthly figure was never reported at all. August's numbers are August alone — 708,211 cars, a normal summer trough — not the two months added together. I have left a gap in the chart rather than interpolating one, because a made-up number that looks like the others is worse than a visible absence.

## What I could not get, and why it matters

I wanted a third chart — battery-electric registrations by manufacturer group, to see who is actually converting and who is talking about it. That data is not published anywhere for free. Registrations by powertrain and registrations by manufacturer are published in the same document, in separate tables that are never crossed.

Which is worth knowing, because tables claiming to show exactly that circulate constantly. Every one I chased turned out to be something else wearing the label: a plug-in table including hybrids, presented as pure electric; a chart of distance to emissions targets, measured in grams per kilometre, quoted as if the numbers were percentages. One version had a manufacturer share column where the top ten summed to 106%.

The genuine public record on manufacturers is a handful of figures. BMW Group at 30% of its own sales electric through August, the highest of the large groups — and [BMW's own newsroom](https://www.press.bmwgroup.com/global/article/detail/T0460314EN/neue-klasse-accelerates-growth-in-fully-electric-vehicle-sales:-milestone-of-two-million-achieved) puts its European BEV share at 28% for the first half, which is the same story told by the company itself. Toyota at 11%, up from 5%. Increases of ten points for Mercedes and eight each for Hyundai and Renault, with no absolute numbers attached at all.

That is not a dataset. It is whatever each manufacturer chose to disclose about itself, on its own definition of Europe, for a period of its own choosing. You cannot rank companies with it — which is exactly why the tables that claim to rank them are made of something else.

And "Europe" is not one place. The same eight months produce 21.7% for the twenty-seven EU states, 23.2% if you add Norway, Switzerland, Iceland and the UK, and 29% for August on a definition that includes Norway and Iceland but excludes Britain. All three are correct. Two of them in one paragraph is how you get nonsense.
